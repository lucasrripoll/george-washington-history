01 A VIRGINIA LIFE

Before the presidency

From the colony
to the continent

Washington’s national career began in a local world of land, family, and imperial politics.

George Washington was born on February 22, 1732, in Westmoreland County, Virginia, then a British colony. His family belonged to the planter class, and his early life connected him to the land and labor systems that made plantation wealth possible. He received less formal schooling than some of his future political peers, but learned mathematics, surveying, and practical estate management. In his teens he surveyed land in Virginia’s expanding western frontier, work that taught him to read terrain and brought him into contact with the colony’s political elite.[1]

In 1753, Virginia’s lieutenant governor sent the young Washington to deliver a warning to French forces claiming territory in the Ohio Valley. The mission failed to make the French leave, but it helped bring him public notice. Washington served in Virginia’s militia during the French and Indian War, where he experienced defeat, logistical problems, and the limits of colonial military power. This conflict was part of a larger imperial struggle; its outcome reshaped Britain’s relationship with its North American colonies and contributed to the tensions that later erupted into revolution.[2]

Washington married Martha Dandridge Custis in 1759 and managed Mount Vernon. The marriage brought social standing and access to wealth, while his plantation relied on enslaved labor. He grew wheat and other crops, experimented with production, and participated in Virginia public life. His story therefore begins with both opportunity and coercion: the same plantation society that helped create his status denied basic freedom to the people whose labor sustained it.

02 THE REVOLUTIONARY WAR

Commander in chief · 1775–1783

Holding an army
together through war

After fighting broke out at Lexington and Concord in April 1775, the Second Continental Congress created a Continental Army and selected Washington as commander in chief. He accepted the appointment on June 16, declining a salary and asking Congress to reimburse his expenses. His Virginia background and military experience helped make him a figure who could represent a continental cause, though the army remained a coalition of people with different interests, resources, and ideas about independence.[3]

The war was not a steady march toward victory. Washington’s forces lost New York in 1776 and retreated across New Jersey. The surprise attack at Trenton on December 26 helped restore morale, but the Continental Army continued to face shortages of clothing, food, weapons, and pay. At Valley Forge in the winter of 1777–78, disease and supply failures caused severe suffering. Training under Baron von Steuben improved discipline, but Washington’s command depended on Congress, state governments, foreign aid, and soldiers who repeatedly chose to stay despite hardship.

Black Americans participated in the struggle in varied ways, serving in Patriot and British forces, seeking freedom through military service, and navigating the competing promises made by both sides. Native nations also made strategic choices amid a war that threatened their lands and sovereignty. French military and financial support became decisive after the alliance of 1778. At Yorktown in 1781, American and French forces surrounded British general Charles Cornwallis. The surrender did not instantly end all fighting, but it effectively closed the major military campaign. The 1783 Treaty of Paris recognized the independence of the United States and set its boundaries, including a vast region west of the Appalachians.[4]

Washington’s importance lay not only in victories but in keeping a fragile army functioning long enough for diplomacy and allied power to matter. The war’s outcome created a republic, while leaving unresolved conflicts over slavery, Native sovereignty, political participation, and the reach of the new government.

03 SURRENDERING COMMAND

An unusual ending

The general
returns to private life

On December 23, 1783, Washington appeared before Congress in Annapolis and gave up his military commission. The act mattered because revolutions can replace one ruler with another, and victorious generals can use armies to seize political power. Washington instead returned authority to the civilian Congress that had appointed him. In his address, he described surrendering “the trust committed to me” and asked to retire from public service.[5]

This was a public performance as well as a personal decision. Newspapers and later generations treated the resignation as evidence of republican virtue: a leader who served the public could relinquish power when the work was done. The example shaped Washington’s reputation in the United States and abroad. Yet it did not mean that the new republic had solved the problem of power. The weak national government struggled to raise revenue, regulate trade, and manage disputes among states. Washington himself came to believe that the country needed a stronger framework.

His return to Mount Vernon also had a private economic dimension. The estate was not separate from national politics; it was a large commercial plantation dependent on enslaved people. The celebrated image of the general retiring to his farm can hide the people who continued to labor there without freedom. Remembering the resignation alongside the plantation complicates the neat story of a leader who simply placed liberty above personal interest.

04 DESIGNING A NEW GOVERNMENT

Philadelphia · 1787

From confederation
to Constitution

Under the Articles of Confederation, the United States had a national Congress but no independent executive and limited ability to tax. Financial problems, trade disputes, and unrest—including Shays’ Rebellion in Massachusetts—convinced many political leaders that the system needed revision. In 1787, delegates met in Philadelphia. Washington presided over the Constitutional Convention, lending the gathering prestige and helping its participants conduct difficult debates. He did not write the Constitution alone: delegates proposed, argued over, and compromised on a plan that created a stronger federal government with three branches.

The Constitution distributed authority between states and the national government, and among legislative, executive, and judicial branches. It also embedded compromises over representation and slavery. The document counted three-fifths of enslaved people for apportionment, strengthening the political power of slaveholding states while denying enslaved people political rights. The Constitution did not abolish the transatlantic slave trade; it prohibited Congress from banning its importation before 1808. These provisions show how the new political union was built through agreements that protected slavery even as revolutionary language celebrated liberty.[6]

Washington’s support for ratification helped persuade skeptical Americans that the new framework could work. The Constitution took effect after ratification by nine states in 1788. Its creation was a major institutional achievement, but it did not settle arguments over federal power, individual rights, or who counted as part of “the people.” Those debates continued through the adoption of the Bill of Rights and remain central to interpreting the founding era.

05 THE FIRST PRESIDENCY

President · 1789–1797

An office made
through practice

Washington took the first presidential oath on April 30, 1789, in New York City. The Constitution described the office in broad terms, leaving the first president to establish many working customs. He created a cabinet by consulting the leaders of executive departments, including Thomas Jefferson as secretary of state and Alexander Hamilton as secretary of the treasury. He developed patterns for communicating with Congress and gave the presidency a visible public presence. Later presidents did not have to copy every choice, but his decisions helped establish expectations for the role.[7]

Hamilton’s financial program proposed that the federal government assume state debts, fund the national debt, and establish a national bank. Washington supported the program, which strengthened federal capacity and helped stabilize public credit. It also provoked opposition from people who feared that the government would favor creditors and concentrate power. The argument helped form competing political coalitions, even though Washington disliked parties and warned against factional conflict.

In 1794, resistance to a federal excise tax on whiskey escalated in western Pennsylvania. Washington first used negotiation, then called militia forces to enforce federal law. The rebellion dispersed without a major battle. Supporters viewed the response as proof that the Constitution gave the government power to act; critics worried about the use of force against citizens. The episode demonstrated both the new government’s reach and the contentious consequences of federal policy.

Foreign affairs posed another challenge. When war resumed between Britain and France in 1793, Washington issued a neutrality proclamation, arguing that the young nation should avoid entering the conflict. His policy angered many Americans who favored France, an ally during the Revolution, but kept the United States out of a European war while it remained militarily and financially vulnerable.[8]

Washington left office after two terms in 1797. There was no constitutional two-term limit at the time; the 22nd Amendment established that limit in 1951. His voluntary departure became a powerful example of peaceful transfer, even as subsequent presidents and political movements interpreted his legacy in different ways.

06 LIBERTY AND ENSLAVEMENT

The people behind the estate

A founding ideal
and a human cost

Washington enslaved people from childhood until his death. His wealth, agricultural production, and household depended on forced labor. In 1799, 317 enslaved people lived and worked at Mount Vernon. They were not all legally his to free: some belonged to the Custis estate through Martha Washington’s first marriage, while others were held by Washington himself or rented from neighbors. This distinction shaped what his will could do, but it does not erase his participation in slavery or the daily coercion experienced by enslaved families.[9]

Washington’s surviving records often describe enslaved people through the property categories and labor roles imposed by enslavers. His 1799 list names workers and notes trades, ages, family relationships, and legal ownership. Read critically, the list is both evidence of the plantation’s organization and a reminder that people’s lives were recorded through an owner’s accounting system. Mount Vernon’s research also uses archaeology, census records, letters, and descendant knowledge to recover more of the community’s history.

Washington’s views shifted over time. During the Revolution he expressed discomfort with slavery and opposed the importation of enslaved people. He privately hoped for gradual abolition, but did not free the people he owned during his lifetime. His will directed that those he personally owned be emancipated after Martha’s death, with William Lee, his longtime enslaved valet, freed immediately and provided a pension. This provision applied to 123 people; it could not free the Custis estate’s enslaved population. Martha freed the people Washington owned in 1801, before her death, reportedly out of concern that the delay created danger.[10]

Ona Judge, an enslaved woman who served in the Washington household, escaped from Philadelphia in 1796 and made a life in New Hampshire. Washington tried to have her returned. Her story demonstrates that enslaved people pursued their own freedom, even when the first president’s household used the federal system to claim them. Centering such stories changes the exhibit’s question from “Was Washington a good person?” to “How did the founding’s institutions and ideals coexist with human bondage, and whose actions challenged that contradiction?”

07 MEMORY AND LEGACY

What his impact means

Remembering a
complicated founder

Washington’s influence came from multiple roles: commander, convention presiding officer, first president, and public symbol. His willingness to resign his military command and leave the presidency helped normalize the idea that leadership in a republic should be temporary and accountable. His administration helped the federal government become operational, set precedents for the executive branch, and pursued policies that increased national authority. These precedents were not rules written in stone; they became reference points that later leaders could accept or challenge.

His impact also included choices with lasting costs. The government’s expansion under his presidency took place alongside warfare and treaty-making that facilitated settler expansion onto Indigenous homelands. The Constitution preserved slavery through compromises, and Washington himself remained an enslaver. His Farewell Address urged national unity, warned against permanent foreign entanglements, and cautioned against intense party divisions. It is a primary source for his political ideas, but it is also a public argument written by a leader seeking to shape how Americans would remember his administration.[11]

Historical memory has often elevated Washington as a nearly flawless national father. A digital exhibit can hold admiration and criticism together: he helped secure independence and model civilian authority, while benefiting from slavery and trying to recover an escaped woman he claimed as property. Studying the contradictions does not make the founding irrelevant. It helps explain how the United States was built, why its promises were limited, and how later generations have struggled to widen the meaning of liberty.

Washington died at Mount Vernon on December 14, 1799. His life remains important not because it offers a simple model to imitate, but because it reveals the challenges of founding a republic amid conflict over power, rights, and freedom. The people who fought, labored, negotiated, resisted, and lived through this era all belong in its history.
