01 THE EARLY YEARS

Before the presidency

From Virginia
to the Revolution

Washington’s path to national leadership began long before the United States existed.

George Washington was born on February 22, 1732, in the colony of Virginia. He grew up in a world shaped by farming and British colonial rule. His formal schooling ended when he was young, but he learned surveying and became familiar with the land and people of Virginia.

As a young man, Washington worked as a surveyor and later served in Virginia’s colonial militia. The French and Indian War gave him military experience, including hard lessons about leadership, logistics, and the dangers of battle. These early years helped prepare him for a much larger conflict.

Washington married Martha Dandridge Custis in 1759 and managed Mount Vernon, his Virginia estate. He became a wealthy planter whose income depended on the labor of enslaved people. This is an essential part of his story: Washington enslaved people throughout much of his life, even as the nation he helped establish declared that all men were created equal.

02 THE REVOLUTIONARY WAR

Commander in chief · 1775–1783

Leading a fight
for independence

In 1775, as the colonies moved toward war with Great Britain, the Continental Congress chose Washington to command the Continental Army. His experience and reputation helped unite soldiers from different colonies under one commander. The army faced shortages, illness, and repeated defeats, and Washington had to keep it together through years of uncertainty.

The winter at Valley Forge in 1777–1778 became a symbol of the army’s hardship. Soldiers endured cold, hunger, and disease, while military training helped make the force more disciplined. Washington’s leadership mattered, but independence came through the efforts and sacrifices of many people, including soldiers, women, free and enslaved Black people, Native communities, and foreign allies.

In 1781, American and French forces surrounded the British army at Yorktown, Virginia. The British surrender there effectively ended major fighting. After the Treaty of Paris recognized American independence in 1783, Washington gave up his military command and returned to private life at Mount Vernon—a decision that strengthened the idea that military power should answer to civilian government.

03 BUILDING A GOVERNMENT

First president · 1789–1797

A new office,
without a rulebook

In 1787, Washington presided over the Constitutional Convention in Philadelphia. Delegates debated how a stronger national government should work, and the Constitution they produced replaced the weaker Articles of Confederation. Washington’s support for the new framework helped build confidence in it, though Americans continued to argue over the government’s powers.

Washington became the first president under the Constitution in 1789. He helped establish the basic working practices of the executive branch, including a cabinet of advisers and regular communication with Congress. Because there was no earlier president to copy, his decisions became precedents that later leaders considered, challenged, or changed.

His administration worked to organize the new federal government and manage its finances. Washington supported creating a national bank and paying national and state debts, policies championed by Treasury Secretary Alexander Hamilton. These choices helped strengthen federal authority, but they also sparked fierce debate about the proper size and reach of the national government.

Washington’s presidency faced conflict at home and abroad. In 1794, he sent forces to suppress the Whiskey Rebellion, showing that the new government intended to enforce federal law. He also issued a neutrality proclamation as war spread between Britain and France, seeking to keep the young country out of a costly European conflict.

After two terms, Washington chose not to seek a third. His 1796 Farewell Address warned about deep political divisions and urged care in foreign alliances. His departure helped establish the expectation that a president could leave office peacefully and voluntarily; the two-term limit later became law through the 22nd Amendment.

04 AN ENDURING, COMPLEX LEGACY

What his impact means

More than
a founding father

Washington’s most lasting impact was helping the United States survive its earliest tests. As commander, he led the army through a long war; as president, he helped make a new constitutional government function. His example of surrendering power—first his command, then the presidency—became an important symbol of republican leadership.

His legacy is also deeply complicated. Washington enslaved hundreds of people over the course of his life and benefited from their forced labor at Mount Vernon. In his will, he arranged for the people he personally owned to be freed after Martha Washington’s death; the people enslaved by the Custis estate were not his to free. His actions and choices must be understood alongside the country’s founding ideals and the people denied those rights.

Washington died at Mount Vernon on December 14, 1799. He remains central to the nation’s memory, but studying him well means looking at both his influence and his contradictions. His story helps explain how the early United States was built—and why its promises of liberty have continued to be debated and expanded.
