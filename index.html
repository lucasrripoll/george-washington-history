01 A VIRGINIA LIFE

Before the presidency

From the colony
to the continent

On February 22, 1732, in Westmoreland County, Virginia, George Washington was born to Mary Ball Washington. He was the eldest of six children from his father's second marriage. He did not grow into a life of royalty or riches, but into a family that belonged to the planter class. Being a part of the planter class meant that his early life connected him to the labor systems that made plantation wealth possible. At the age of 11, George Washington lost his father. This affected many aspects of his childhood, including his educational opportunities. Growing up, he received less formal schooling, but still learned mathematics, surveying, and practical estate management. As he grew up, Virginia was expanding their western frontier. So, in his teens, he surveyed land in Virginia, which taught him how to read terrain and begin to network with some of his colony's elite political leaders.[1][12]

George Washington's military career began before the American Revolution. In 1753, he was serving in Virginia's militia, and a Virginia lieutenant governor sent Washington to deliver a message to the French forces who were claiming territory in the Ohio Valley: to stop. Albeit the message was not listened to, it helped bring George Washington notice within his community. He continued to serve in Virginia's militia in the French and Indian War, where he learned how to be a leader, experienced defeat, and also overcame adversity and logistical problems. The French and Indian conflict was part of a larger imperial struggle, and the outcome of the war changed Britain's relationship with the colonies and added to the already high tensions.[2]

In 1759, Washington married Martha Dandridge Custis, who was a mother of two children. George and Martha did not have any children of their own, but they raised her children and later their grandchildren. Because of their marriage, social standing and access to wealth became readily accessible to Washington. During his time with Martha, George Washington developed Mount Vernon, a large plantation, and became involved in business, farming, and land management. He grew wheat and other crops, as well as experimenting with production. On top of developing his plantation, he entered politics by serving in Virginia's House of Burgesses. Here, his story begins with opportunity and coercion: the very thing making him rich and building his status was also denying freedom to those who labored for him[13]

02 THE REVOLUTIONARY WAR

Commander in chief · 1775–1783

Holding an army
together through war

In April of 1775, fighting broke out at Lexington and Concord. During this time, the Second Continental Congress created a Continental Army and appointed George Washington as the commander-in-chief. On June 16th, Washington accepted the appointment without a salary, but instead asked Congress to support him and reimburse his expenses. Because of his experience in the military and his job in Virginia as a land surveyor, Washington was well prepared to represent the Continental cause, albeit his army remained a group of people with differing interests and ideas about independence.[3]

This war would be no easy feat for Washington. In 1776, his forces lost New York to the British and had to retreat across New Jersey. However, George Washington had his wins. On December 26, Washington launched a surprise attack against the Hessians during his famous Delaware River crossing. This victory helped restore the Continental Army's morale, but they continued to face adversities such as low ammunition and pay, shortages of clothing, and not enough food. One of the most difficult periods for Washington's army was during the winter of 1777 - 1778. While his army camped at Valley Forge, Pennsylvania, his soldiers were met with freezing temperatures, poor shelter, outbreaks of disease, and not enough clothing. Thousands of his troops became ill, and many of them died. Nevertheless, Washington stayed with his troops and worked hard to uplift their spirits and raise morale. During this time, Baron Friedrich von Steuben, trained his soldiers and disciplined them in battlefield tactics, which helped transform the Continental Army into a real threat.[14]

African Americans participated in the war and struggled in many different ways. There were Black Americans serving on both the Patriot and British forces, all seeking freedom from slavery through military service. African Americans participated in the war in various ways. Some fought in the Patriot or British armies, while others sought out their freedom by fighting for a side or faced the hazards and promises of the service of each side.[15] Native nations made significant choices as well as they fought to keep their own lands and their independence in a war that posed a deadly threat to them.[16] French aid in cash and troops proved decisive once they entered the war after the 1778 alliance.[17] As the British retreated from the south to the Yorktown peninsula in 1781, American and French armies trapped British general Charles Cornwallis' forces. Cornwallis' surrender didn't immediately end the fighting, but it did mark the end of major hostilities. In 1783 the Treaty of Paris acknowledged the sovereignty of the newly created United States of America and established its borders, which included a huge expanse of land west of the Appalachian Mountains.[4] Washington's significance, however, was not his victories. He managed to hold a delicate army in place long enough for his and America's allies to offer diplomatic aid and assistance. The revolution built a new republic. However, it also left unresolved issues including slavery, Native sovereignty, political participation, and who would hold the real power of the new government. On December 23, 1783, Washington appeared before Congress at Annapolis. There, he turned over the soldiers' sword, and closed the military chapter of his life. George Washington’s impact was significant because his revolution could have changed regimes and could have taken power. Instead, Washington turned over his power to the ones that had placed him in authority. Washington spoke of "delivering the trust committed to me" and asked to set down his sword in peace.[5]

03 SURRENDERING COMMAND

An unusual ending

The general
returns to private life

His resignation was, on one level, a personal act. However, it was also seen by newspapers and by historians to come as a test case of republican virtue: a ruler whose work was done and could lay down his power. Washington's resignation helped to establish his reputation, not only in the United States, but everywhere else his name was known. But he didn’t retire from public life. The new republic still couldn’t figure out how to rid itself of the age-old problem of power. The government was having trouble raising revenue, overseeing trade, and resolving interstate conflicts. Washington increasingly thought the country needed a new approach.[5]

Going back to Mount Vernon also meant going back to plantation life. The estate was integrated into the nation's political and economic spheres, and it relied on the labor of those who were enslaved. The usual picture of Washington returning to his farm might omit the people who had to keep working there. Knowing that Washington had done the noble thing in retiring but still coming back to plantation life complicates his story of a leader who prioritized liberty over self-interest.[9]

04 DESIGNING A NEW GOVERNMENT

Philadelphia · 1787

From confederation
to Constitution

The United States had a Congress under the Articles of Confederation, but lacked an independent executive branch and had weak financial resources. Lack of the ability to tax, along with financial difficulties, trade problems, and unrest (notably, Shays' Rebellion in Massachusetts) led many to want a change. In 1787, delegates met at the Constitutional Convention in Philadelphia. During this convention, George Washington was elected president of said convention. His support brought prestige to the convention and helped the delegates through many difficult votes. Thus the constitution was formed. Many people believe it was all Washington and his ideas. However, people forget that the delegates put forth ideas, argued, and finally compromised to form a stronger central government with three branches.[6]

The Constitution divided power between state and national government, as well as between the legislative, executive, and judicial branches of the national government. It also made compromises on the issues of representation and slavery. For example, it did count three-fifths of enslaved people for representation, while at the same time giving the slave-holding states increased political power while depriving enslaved people of political rights. The new constitution also kept the transatlantic slave trade alive by prohibiting Congress from banning the importation of enslaved people until 1808. This was how the new government managed to keep slavery alive even as the nation celebrated its liberty.[6]

Washington was in favor of ratification and helped convince Americans that the new plan would succeed. The new Constitution went into effect when nine states ratified it in 1788. It was a radical change from how the nation was governed, but it didn't end the multitude of questions people had, including questions about the extent of federal power, the limits on individual rights, and who was included in the phrase "the People." Those questions persisted beyond the Bill of Rights and remain an ongoing feature of how Americans interpret the founding era.[6]

05 THE FIRST PRESIDENCY

President · 1789–1797

An office made
through practice

Washington took the oath of office for president for the first time on April 30, 1789 in New York City. Since the new Constitution was very vague about the role of president, Washington had to figure out many of the president's traditions. Before Washington, there was never a president before. He had no one to look back to for advice or to see how to even be a good president. But, nevertheless, he took action. Washington assembled a cabinet by turning to the heads of the different executive departments-such as Thomas Jefferson, who served as secretary of state, and Alexander Hamilton, who was appointed secretary of the treasury-set rules for communicating with Congress, and made the president an accessible, recognizable part of the American scene. Because of the actions he took, presidents to come would have someone to look back to for advice and to see how to be a president.[7]

Hamilton's financial plan was to have the federal government assume state debts, create a new national debt, and form a national bank. Washington thought this was a great idea and approved. Hamilton’s plan gave the federal government more power and stabilized public credit. However, not everyone was happy. Critics opposed it because they said it favored creditors over debtors and that it would give too much power to the federal government. This argument led to the development of competing political parties, which Washington was not fond of.[7]

In 1794, a protest against a federal tax on whiskey erupted in western Pennsylvania. Washington wanted to resolve the issue with negotiations and militia troops to suppress the disorder. The rebellion was squashed without war breaking out. His supporters celebrated the use of force as a demonstration that the new government had the authority to use the power given to them in the Constitution. His opponents however were concerned that the government might use its power against its own people.[7]

Another issue Washington faced involved foreign affairs. When Britain and France went back to war in 1793, Washington issued a neutrality proclamation. He believed that the U.S. being a new country should avoid going into the fighting. Since many Americans had been allies of the French during the Revolution, this policy upset a lot of Americans. However, it kept the United States out of a European war while the new country was weak financially and militarily.[8]

In 1797, Washington resigned after two terms. No one told him he couldn’t run again. In fact, there was no two-term limit in the Constitution. It was added by the 22nd Amendment in 1951. Still, his decision to step down set an important precedent for a peaceful transfer of power.[7]

06 LIBERTY AND ENSLAVEMENT

The people behind the estate

A founding ideal
and a human cost

Enslaved people lived, grew, and cooked at Mount Vernon from birth until their deaths. Washington depended on their labor for his wealth, on his farms, and in his house. By 1799, 317 people lived and worked on the Mount Vernon estate. Not all could be freed by Washington's will because some of them belonged to the Custis estate through Martha Washington's first husband. Some Washington owned directly and others were rented from neighbors. This made some difference in who Washington's will could free. Still, it doesn’t change the fact that he depended on slavery or the burdens that he placed upon enslaved families.[9]

In Washington's records, the names those enslaved under Washington emerge only as property and as labor. His 1799 list includes the names, trades, ages, relationships, and legal ownership of all those who were enslaved. Washington’s list reveals the framework of the plantation, and also reminds us that the lives of enslaved people are preserved in the ledger of an owner's accounting. Mount Vernon's study incorporates archaeology, records, and descendants' expertise to help deepen our understanding of the community living in Mt. Vernon.[9]

While the Revolution was underway Washington spoke out against slavery, and was against importing slaves to the colonies.[19] He had hoped for a gradual end to slavery,[18] but didn’t free any of the slaves he owned during his lifetime. After he died, his will ordered them to be emancipated after Martha's death, but freed his, and hers, and William Lee, his personal enslaved valet, entirely, immediately, and with a pension. This applied to 123 people, not including those who belonged to the estate of Martha's first husband. She freed her husband's and her own slaves in 1801 right before she died because she was scared that they would be in danger after she died.

Ona Judge, an enslaved woman in Washington’s household, escaped Philadelphia in 1796 and settled in New Hampshire. Washington did attempt to reclaim her. Her story proves slaves also wanted to be free, even in the home of the first president. By sharing her story we complicate the age-old question of "Was Washington a good guy?" by considering "How did the founding ideals co-exist with slavery, and how did people challenge it?"[10]

07 MEMORY AND LEGACY

What his impact means

Remembering a
complicated founder

Washington was a man in various positions. He was a military leader, the president of the Constitutional Convention, the first president, and a public icon. Through his many different roles and positions, he contributed to the form of leadership. By resigning his command of the army and stepping down as president, Washington helped define the now dominant concept that leadership in a republic should be in limited time and that one should be accountable for one's actions. His presidency also defined the executive branch and expanded the authority of the national government.[6][7]

Washington's Decisions. As the government expanded during his presidency, war and treaties moved towards Indigenous lands.[16] Slavery was still prominent because it was engraved in the Constitution through its compromises,[6] and Washington was himself a slave owner. In Washington's Farewell Address,[11] he called for national unity, warned of the problems permanent foreign alliances could cause, and spoke against severe party divisions. The address was written by a man who wanted to guide his nation into the future and sheds light on how our first president wanted to be remembered.

Americans have long thought of Washington as a near-perfect national father and first president. However, no man is perfect. He was the essence of a revolutionary, and a perfect example of what a president should be. But, he was also a slave owner, and he pursued a runaway who had escaped his household. Examining these differences in Washington can shed light on the history of our nation and the narrow lense of its promises, as well as how new generations have attempted to re-define liberty.[10]

On December 14, 1799, Washington died at his beloved Mount Vernon. His life doesn’t just matter because there are lessons we can take away. It matters because we can look at his life and see the struggles he faced. Struggles of establishing a republic in the face of adversity, as well as of a challenge to that rule, power, rights and freedom. Every person who fought, worked, negotiated and struggled contributed to that history.[1]
